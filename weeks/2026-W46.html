The week in five minutes

  1. Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia. Within five days of admitting an affair with a Megaspeed executive whom US intelligence links to China's Ministry of State Security, the founder resigned as CEO and left the board on Thursday; CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO. Why it matters: the loudest voice against US chip curbs on China is gone, a week before a US–China summit in Shenzhen.
  2. Trump walked away from the Iran talks. "We don't need a deal," he said on Thursday, while the land and naval blockade stays in place and Iran's chief negotiator refuses to ship out "a single speck" of enriched uranium. Why it matters: the war moves from bombs to economic strangulation, and the Gulf states start hedging.
  3. Hot US inflation pushed the 10-year above 5.1%. October CPI rose 0.4% and core 0.3%, both above forecast; the 10-year yield climbed about 30 basis points on the week to 5.15%, and markets price a Fed hike on 9 December. Why it matters: the Treasury Secretary is now publicly arguing with the Fed, a sign that the debt is setting the rate path.
  4. Bitcoin fell about 15% to near $64,600. It was the sixth weekly loss in a row; US spot ETFs lost about $1.07 billion and investors locked in $680 million of losses in one day. Why it matters: Bitcoin is now below the price at which Strategy, the largest holder, last bought.
  5. Stocks rose anyway. The S&P 500 gained 2.5%, the SMI 2.1%; gold fell 4.6% to about $3,886 as yields and the dollar rose, and the franc lost 2.8% against the dollar. Why it matters: money is choosing yield and the dollar over hedges, for now.
  6. The SVP delayed its Bundesrat shortlist. The selection committee did not name its favourites on 13 November as planned; the parliamentary group still aims to nominate on 20 November, ahead of the 9 December election.

Geopolitics

The Nvidia affair is a US–China story, not a gossip story

widely covered

Fact The week began with Nvidia's board opening an independent review after Jensen Huang admitted an extramarital affair with Alice Huang (no relation), an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore firm that bought about $2 billion of Nvidia products and that US officials suspect of routing chips to China. President Trump first backed him and said the FBI was investigating; The New York Times then reported Trump had been told of the affair in May, and Axios that the commerce secretary had received a memo. After reports of a second relationship, with a vice president who reported to him, Huang resigned on Thursday, effective immediately. Beijing called the case part of a US tech "blockade"; Bloomberg reports the woman now lives in Beijing with the knowledge of Chinese security officials. Commerce is set to close the loophole that lets Chinese firms rent US chips in third-country data centres, and Republican senators want Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify. Opinion International Intrigue argues espionage is about a "mosaic" of small insights, so the board's "no secrets leaked" framing misses the point; Semafor's Reed Albergotti says Huang's real mistake was getting too involved in Washington. SemiAnalysis warns that tighter "remote access" curbs will push China to build its own chips faster. Claude Both can be true: export controls slow China today and speed its self-reliance tomorrow. The new fact is that the US now treats a chip company's chief executive as a national-security risk, which moves Silicon Valley closer to the status of a defence contractor.

Iran: from talks to strangulation

widely covered

Fact On Monday Trump said the US had "won" the war and that a land blockade of Iran was in effect; after meeting Qatar's prime minister he called the military part "effectively over", while Secretary of State Marco Rubio saw no breakthrough. On Thursday he said the US does not need a deal, ending the talks led by Vice President JD Vance. Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei, Vance's counterpart, said no enriched uranium will leave the country because Iran needs it for nuclear-powered submarines, and Iranian MPs debated leaving the Non-Proliferation Treaty. OPEC output rose 1.97 million barrels a day in October to near prewar levels as Saudi Arabia pumped more through pipelines that bypass Hormuz; Iran's own output is about half its prewar level. On Friday Türkiye and Qatar signed a mutual defence agreement. Opinion John Mearsheimer argues the midterms will not change Trump's course and that blockade and sanctions ("Iron Gate", "Economic Outcast") hurt Iran without breaking its resolve. Alex Vatanka warns Washington not to mistake Rezaei's prominence for flexibility. Claude Walking away is a bet that time favours the blockader. Iran's answer, a nuclear-threshold debate, raises the cost of that bet, and the Gulf's new defence pacts show allies are no longer sure the US will finish what it started.

Ukraine: talks in Abu Dhabi, envoys in Moscow, spies in Britain

Fact US, Russian and Ukrainian negotiators met in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday and Wednesday on a truce for energy infrastructure and Black Sea shipping; President Zelensky called the first day "constructive", but neither side made concessions and both kept striking. Politico reports Washington asked whether Moscow would accept a NATO-like Western guarantee for Ukraine. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner then met Vladimir Putin in Moscow, and Ukraine reported a new Starlink outage hampering its army. In Britain, police arrested a 41-year-old US Army soldier at NATO's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps headquarters on suspicion of spying for Russia; Britain and Estonia both called Russia a "state sponsor of terrorism". Europeans are pressing for a seat at the next round, and Euronews reports Washington and Kyiv now support that. Opinion Mearsheimer says Ukraine cannot win and that Europe keeping it in the fight is "strategically bankrupt"; Norway's prime minister, who spoke to Putin last month, says Putin is not interested in peace. Claude The two views meet in one place: Russia's gains have stalled, so both sides think time is on their side. A truce on energy and shipping is the realistic first step, not a peace.

Switzerland

Bundesrat race: the SVP needs another week

Fact The SVP's selection committee under Caspar Baader was due to name its favourites for Guy Parmelin's seat on 13 November, but postponed the decision to next week without giving a reason, watson reports. Eight people are running, among them Céline Amaudruz, Marco Chiesa, Pierre Alain Schnegg, Franz Ruppen, Michael Graber, Rémy Wyssmann, Jean-Pierre Gallati and group leader Thomas Aeschi, who declared late. The parliamentary group still plans to nominate its ticket on 20 November; the Federal Assembly elects on 9 December and is not bound by the party's choice. Claude A delay this late usually means the committee is checking candidates' records more closely. A French-speaking seat and a two-name ticket are the SVP's own stated aims, which narrows the field more than the long list suggests.

29 November: four federal votes

Fact Voters decide on extra AHV funding through a higher VAT (a constitutional change that also needs a majority of cantons), the "fairness" initiative, the fireworks initiative and a loosening of the war-material export law. A YouGov poll from October had the AHV-VAT plan behind (39% yes, 51% no), the fireworks initiative ahead (62% yes) and the war-material law open, with almost three in ten undecided. Claude The war-material vote is the one with geopolitical weight: it decides whether Swiss arms makers can sell more freely to countries rearming because of Ukraine and the Gulf war.

SNB sees no credit crunch; the franc weakens on Fed bets

Fact The SNB says domestic lending has grown steadily, including after Credit Suisse, and it sees no sign of a credit crunch; it expects growth of 1.5–2% this year and about 1.5% in 2027. Swiss inflation rose from 0.6% in May to 1.5% in October, mainly from oil products, still inside the 0–2% target; the policy rate is 0%. The franc lost about 2.8% against the dollar this week as markets priced a Fed hike. Claude A wider rate gap with the US eases the strong-franc problem for exporters, but it imports some of the oil-driven inflation the SNB is watching.

Money & Macro

Hot inflation, a 5% bond yield, and a Treasury Secretary leaning on the Fed

widely covered

Fact US consumer prices rose 0.4% in October and core prices 0.3%, both a tenth above forecasts; no annual rate was published because October 2025 prices were not collected during the shutdown. Before the report, futures put the chance of a quarter-point hike at the 8–9 December meeting above 80%, and Governor Christopher Waller said core inflation near 0.3% could justify half a point. The US 10-year yield rose about 30 basis points on the week to 5.15%. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on CNBC that hiking "too much into an energy supply shock" would be a mistake. Ray Dalio lists the funding deadlines: government money runs out on 11 December, the debt limit is projected for March 2027. Opinion James Lavish (The Informationist) calls Bessent's campaign "fiscal dominance, right out in the open": with $40.5 trillion of debt and $103 billion of interest paid in October alone, higher rates hit the Treasury first. Dalio expects "more fighting and more dysfunction" and higher bill yields around each deadline. Claude The Fed has hiked once and is likely to again; the open question is whether it keeps going once the interest bill becomes the political issue. Stocks rose this week regardless, a sign the market believes the Fed will blink first.

The AI trade after Huang: $1 trillion lost, and an IPO six times oversubscribed

Fact Nvidia lost nearly $1 trillion of market value within days and fell more than 14% intraday on Thursday before closing about 4% lower; it reports earnings on 17 November. Neoclouds Nebius and CoreWeave fell as much as 19% and 9%, and SoftBank took a $3.7 billion hit on cancelled gas-power orders. Yet Anthropic's IPO, aimed at late next week at a valuation the FT puts at up to $1.5 trillion, is reported six times oversubscribed; Goldman Sachs, a lead bank, forecasts revenue above $600 billion by 2030. JPMorgan sees US AI capex of at least $1.5 trillion in 2027, then flat. Opinion Michael Burry calls Huang "the Enforcer" of a coercive sales model and doubts any successor replaces him. Doomberg argues the bigger threat to US AI dominance is that China builds data centres and power plants faster and cheaper. Claude Demand for compute is not the question; who controls the supply chain and at what political cost is. A record IPO in a week of CEO scandal says investors separate the two, which is rational only if the power and permits arrive on time.

Gold: down 4.6% as yields and the dollar rose

Fact Gold fell 4.6% on the week to about $3,886 an ounce, giving back last week's gain; in francs the fall was smaller, to about CHF 3,258, because the dollar rose 2.8% against the franc. The drop came as the US 10-year rose about 30 basis points and markets priced a December Fed hike. Claude Gold is behaving as a real-rate asset again: when yields jump, it falls, whatever the geopolitics. For a CHF holder the franc's weakness cushioned most of the move, which is the practical reason to measure gold in francs, not dollars.

The Austrian reading Claude

Definition. Fiscal dominance is when a government's debt is so large that the central bank's rate decisions follow the Treasury's financing needs rather than the price level. Austrians see it as the late stage of credit expansion, when the state becomes the marginal borrower that cannot afford positive real rates.

  1. The burden. The US owes over $40.5 trillion; $12 trillion must be refinanced by the end of 2027, and October's interest bill was $103 billion, The Informationist reports from Treasury data.
  2. The pressure. Each hike raises that bill quickly, so the Treasury Secretary now argues in public against more hikes, calling the inflation an energy "supply shock".
  3. The outcome. In Mises' terms, a central bank that protects the state's borrowing costs keeps money cheaper than savings justify. The price of that is paid through a weaker currency, which is why gold and Bitcoin are bought as exits from the chain.

Counter-argument. The Fed hiked in September despite Bessent and is likely to hike again in December, so its independence is bent, not broken. A supply shock from war can fade by itself, and raising rates into it can cause a recession without fixing prices. And this week both gold and Bitcoin fell while yields rose, so the "exit" has not yet worked in the short run.

Bitcoin

Price and flows: −15%, the sixth weekly loss in a row

Fact Bitcoin traded near $64,600 (CHF 54,100) on Sunday, down about 15% from last Sunday and below its October low of about $77,300. It fell from about $76,000 at the start of the week to a low near $62,600 early on Sunday. US spot Bitcoin ETFs lost about $1.07 billion from 9 to 13 November, with outflows on every day, the second week in a row above $1 billion; they are now about $2 billion negative for 2026. About $680 million of futures positions were liquidated in 24 hours, mostly longs. Claude The drop came with rising yields, a stronger dollar and the AI wobble, not with any Bitcoin-specific news. That makes it a liquidity sell-off; such phases end when forced sellers run out, not when the news improves.

Treasuries and miners: Strategy buys, miners sell for AI

Fact Strategy bought 1,036 BTC at an average $79,012 and passed 850,000 BTC (850,364 as of 8 November), while spending $188.3 million buying back its STRC preferred. Miners sold: Riot Platforms cut its holdings 79% in the third quarter, from 11,380 to 2,418 BTC, to fund a 910-megawatt AI data-centre lease worth about $28 billion; Keel Infrastructure (formerly Bitfarms) sold all 2,469 BTC, Hut 8 sold 4,066. Tether's CEO said the company owns about 150,000 BTC, about 50,000 more than its reserve attestation shows. Unconfirmed The extra 50,000 Tether coins are a claim, not a disclosure. Claude Miners turning into AI landlords is the week's structural signal: the most natural long-term holders are becoming sellers, and their hashrate and power now go to whoever pays more.

On-chain and sentiment: panic sellers lock in losses

Fact On Thursday investors realised more than $680 million of losses on-chain, mostly coins bought above $80,000, Checkonchain reports. Regulation: in the EU, a lawyer with Bitcoin Austria is challenging the DAC8 reporting rules that cover self-hosted wallets, Was Bitcoin bringt reports. Practical insight 1 · architecture

Let an assistant check your Aktennotiz against the meeting

The Rundown's Rowan Cheung records calls and asks Claude afterwards for every commitment made and every decision put off. The same works for a Bauherren- or Planersitzung: record (with consent), get a transcript, and have the assistant compare it with your draft Aktennotiz. It catches promises that never made it into the minutes. Ask for exact quotes, so each item can be checked against what was said.

"Here is the transcript of today's planning meeting and my draft Aktennotiz. List every decision, open point and commitment (who, what, by when) in the transcript, quote the sentence it comes from, and mark which ones are missing or worded differently in my draft. Output a table: item · owner · deadline · quote · in draft yes/no."

Practical insight 2 · resilience

Keep a local copy of what you cannot lose

Friday's outage stopped Outlook and Teams across offices. For project work, keep current plans, the latest Werkvertrag and contact lists exported to a local or second-cloud folder each week, and export a copy before any large AI-assisted change. It costs minutes and turns an outage into a nuisance.

The bigger picture

When companies become fronts Claude

Great powers. This week a chipmaker's chief executive fell because Washington now judges a private company's leadership by national-security standards. That is what happens in great-power rivalry: the state reaches into the firms that carry its edge, as Britain did with its chartered companies and the US with defence contractors after 1945. China did this long ago; the US is catching up.

Technology. Power and permits are becoming the binding limit on AI, not chips alone. China builds data centres in Inner Mongolia faster and cheaper, while US voters reject them at the ballot box and Congress tries to rescue them through permitting reform.

Society. Spain's housing strike and the Dutch wealth-tax fight show the same pressure: when assets rise faster than wages, politics turns to who owns what.

Economy. A Treasury arguing with its central bank, yields above 5%, and gold and Bitcoin falling together point to a tight-dollar phase. History suggests such phases end with the central bank giving way, but often only after something breaks.

What to learn. Concentration is the common risk this week: one CEO, one cloud provider, one strait. Spread what you depend on.

Contradictions, blind spots & important additions

Was she a spy?

US intelligence links Alice Huang to China's Ministry of State Security; Semafor's technology desk notes the allegations are unproven and she "may not even be a spy". The resignation followed the affairs and the governance failure, not a proven leak. Claude

More oil, more attacks

OPEC output is back near prewar levels while Iranian attacks around Hormuz have picked up. Both are true: Saudi oil now leaves through pipelines and Red Sea ports, which works only while those are spared. Claude

Blind spot: Europe's gas

Coverage focused on Nvidia, but Europe enters winter with low gas stocks and expects to leave it with even less; a cold spell would hit industry and inflation, including in Switzerland. Claude
