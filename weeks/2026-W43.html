The week in five minutes

  1. The Gulf war spread to Saudi soil again. Brent hit its highest level since early May (about $114 intraday on Friday). Over the weekend the Houthis claimed strikes on Riyadh's airport and Saudi oil sites, and Trump said the US "may do another little Epic Fury". Why it matters: oil is the main driver of this year's inflation and of every central bank decision this autumn.
  2. France's borrowing costs hit a 24-year high. The 10-year yield briefly topped 5% on Wednesday, with the gap to Germany near 160 basis points, the widest since 2011. Moody's held the rating on Friday. Why it matters: a debt scare in the eurozone's second economy weighs on the euro and pushes money into the franc.
  3. Wall Street swapped AI stocks for bonds. The Nasdaq fell 2.6% on Thursday; the US 10-year closed the week at 4.99%, down 11 basis points. Why it matters: the Fed decides on Wednesday, and Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon report the same week.

Geopolitics & World

Gulf: the war hits refineries and airports

widely covered

Fact The US–Israeli air campaign against Iran began on 28 February. An 8 April ceasefire ended the heaviest fighting, and a June memorandum meant to lead to a settlement later collapsed. Since then Iran claims control of the Strait of Hormuz and attacks ships that cross without its permission, while the US keeps a naval blockade on Iranian ports.

This week the pressure moved to Saudi Arabia. Brent reached its highest level since 4 May after Houthi attacks on the kingdom. On Saturday the Houthis said they hit Riyadh's King Khalid airport and Saudi oil facilities; on Sunday Saudi air defences intercepted drones and missiles over Riyadh and ten ballistic missiles aimed at the south. Riyadh's airport had only just reopened two terminals after a 10 October strike that killed at least 12 people.

Diplomacy did not move. Foreign Minister Araghchi said on Sunday that a new war would not change Iran's position and that Hormuz's shipping routes and management "will change" permanently. Iranian media denied reports that Tehran had sent a reopening offer through Pakistani mediators. On Friday Trump said Washington "may do another little Epic Fury", the name of the original campaign.

Second-order: Doomberg's October Pro note argues that attacks on civilian energy infrastructure, refineries above all, have become normal. Claude That makes a shortage of refined products (diesel, jet fuel) more likely than a crude shortage. It also explains why Washington lifted sanctions on Russian diesel for six months earlier this month, and why the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is at a 40-year low (The Bitcoin Layer).

Source ABC News · Doomberg: Academy – Refining · Crypto Times: BTC below $80,000 · Wikipedia

France: from street protests to a bond scare

widely covered

Fact Protests drew about 550,000 people on 18 October, rioters burned Limoges's town hall, and more than 600 rallies were planned for 24 October. On Wednesday the French 10-year yield briefly passed 5.00% for the first time since July 2002, and the spread over German Bunds neared 160 basis points, the widest since the 2011 euro crisis. ING says parliament is positioning for elections instead of fixing the 2027 budget. Moody's had been expected to cut France from Aa3 to A1 on Friday; it kept the rating and said it expects the deficit to shrink in 2027.

The damage is reaching Switzerland: Breitling's CEO said the unrest has "devastated" its French business (swissinfo).

Opinion Peter McCormack condemns the violence but reads it as young people reacting to debt and housing costs loaded onto them by older voters. Friedrich & Partner's Sunday letter asks whether a "euro crisis 2.0" is coming and whether Germany pays (body not readable, subject line only). Tucker Carlson's episode with a former ICE official frames the riots through the "Great Replacement" theory; that is a political claim, not an established fact. All-In discusses "the fall of France" from a VC perspective.

Source Democrata/ING · abcbourse: Moody's and France · Mises "Notes from the Margin" · Euronews

Ukraine: Witkoff in St. Petersburg, Starlink back on

Fact US envoy Steve Witkoff met Putin on 21 October, arriving from Oslo where he had seen Zelensky. He urged a return to three-way talks and thanked Putin for the diesel deal. No agreement followed: Lavrov ruled out political concessions, and Moscow rejects a full ceasefire. Zelensky said Ukraine and the US had agreed proposals including security guarantees and that US intelligence sharing has resumed. On Sunday Ukraine said Starlink and Starshield were back to full speed after more than a week of throttling that disrupted drone defence. Reuters reports US administrations have long asked Musk to switch the service on or off by country.

Switzerland

Federal Council race: seven in, Aeschi undecided

Fact The SVP's deadline closed on Friday at noon. Declared: Céline Amaudruz (GE), Marco Chiesa (TI), Rémy Wyssmann (SO), Michael Graber and Franz Ruppen (both VS), Pierre Alain Schnegg (BE) and Jean-Pierre Gallati (AG). Group leader Thomas Aeschi (ZG) was nominated by his canton but says he will decide "in the coming weeks". Thomas Matter withdrew, and the Zurich party, the SVP's largest, fields no one.

Amaudruz is the most debated name. She breaks with her group on social issues such as same-sex marriage and paternity leave, which could win left-wing votes in December but cost her inside the party. Roger Köppel (Weltwoche, opinion) called a nomination of her a "monumental mistake". Next: hearings with selection chair Caspar Baader, a proposal by 13 November, the group's ticket around 20 November, and the vote on 9 December, where Parliament is not bound by the ticket.

Source SRF: Diese SVP-Kandidaten gehen ins Bundesratsrennen · bluewin: Amaudruz is becoming a problem · Tages-Anzeiger

FINMA approves the first retail spot Bitcoin ETF

Fact BBIT from Zurich-based BIT Asset Management will list on SIX in November. Swiss investors have had Bitcoin ETPs since 2018, which are legally debt notes. BBIT is a fund under collective-investment law, with FINMA waiving the usual diversification rule. Fee and custodian are not yet public. Claude Easier access helps adoption; the trade-off is unchanged, since a fund share is a claim on a custodian.

Source Relai · BeInCrypto: Switzerland's first spot Bitcoin ETF

Money, Macro & Bitcoin

Rates: a near three-sigma bond rally

Fact The Bitcoin Layer calls this week's fall in US yields a near three-sigma move: money left the Nasdaq for Treasuries, and options traders are again paying up for bets on further bond gains, the first time since early 2026. Its reasons: oil keeps inflation high, the SPR is at a 40-year low ahead of the midterms, and the weaker half of the "K-shaped" economy is cracking. US pending home sales posted their worst yearly change since August 2023; Mises notes record auto-loan delinquencies and the worst card delinquencies among over-70s since 2011.

The Fed (3.75–4.00% after a September hike) meets on 27–28 October. CME pricing on Thursday put a second hike at under 10%. Opinion Dalio's 13 October note argues the bond bear market "probably has further to run" as Treasury supply rises and foreign demand fades. One rally does not settle which of the two is right.

Source The Bitcoin Layer: Is The AI Trade Rolling Over? · Ray Dalio: The Numbers Behind the Bond Bear Market · Mises Wire: Market Coiling · CryptoTicker: Bitcoin and the Fed window

The Austrian reading Claude

Definition. For Austrians, a bubble is malinvestment: capital tied up in projects that only looked profitable because credit was cheap. The bust is when those projects are revealed and liquidated.

  1. The boom. Years of low rates and easy credit funded long-dated bets: AI data centres financed with debt, housing bought at 3% mortgages, auto loans stretched to the limit.
  2. The signal. Rates rose (10-year up to 5.36% in early October). New mortgages now cost far more than old ones (TBL's "massive K"), and delinquencies climb first where borrowers are weakest.
  3. The revaluation. Investors start asking where demand for all the compute comes from. This week's Nasdaq drop and the move into Treasuries fit that phase. Mises Wire's pieces on the Panic of 1837 and the 2000s housing bubble make the historical case.
  4. The policy choice. Letting the liquidation happen is painful in an election year with a war on. The Austrian expectation is that governments will eventually choose renewed money creation instead, which is the case for hard money.

Counter-argument. Much AI spending comes from cash-rich firms out of profits, not only borrowed money, and real demand for AI is growing fast (Anthropic's revenue grew roughly fifteenfold in a year). Some of today's "malinvestment" may turn out to be ordinary investment that simply arrived early.

AI

Anthropic's numbers come out ahead of a listing

widely covered

Fact A draft IPO prospectus seen by the Wall Street Journal shows first-half 2026 revenue of $16.3bn, up from $1.1bn a year earlier, and a net loss of $80.7bn. About $72bn of that is an accounting charge tied to convertible notes. Compute commitments total roughly $450bn over 7–10 years, mostly to Google Cloud and AWS. A November IPO is expected; reported valuations range from about $1.5 to $2 trillion. The public filing and exact date are not yet confirmed. Unconfirmed CNBC reports OpenAI is raising up to $30bn at a $1.25tn valuation.

Source WSJ via Investing.com · Reuters via Quartz · Dealroom

The PHP aftermath: AI finds old bugs faster than we patch

Fact The two critical PHP flaws that took government sites offline date back to PHP 5 in 2004. They were found with AI agents (OpenAI's and Google's models, via Calif and Wiz), and details leaked a day before the fix. Censys counted about 1.5m of 2.6m PHP hosts still unpatched. Security experts warn AI is doubling the pace of flaw discovery and shrinking the window to patch. The same week, eight intermediate certificate keys at eMudhra were compromised; pip, uv and certifi shipped updated trust lists.

Source Simon Willison: PHP security release · DEV digest · TechCrunch · All-In

Practical insight 1

"Make it better" loops, with a check

Ethan Mollick built his book charts by giving an agent short guidance and then repeatedly asking it to improve the result. The AI caught a math error of his own. Use the same loop, but force a check each round:

"Improve this. Then list every number you used, where it came from, and one thing that could be wrong. Don't change anything I marked as final."

Example: drafting a cost comparison for a building project or a CHF budget. The source list each round is what catches the "fluent but wrong" answer.

Practical insight 2

Test an AI tool with bad inputs

SAP's CTO suggests feeding an agent inputs your real system would reject, such as an invoice with no currency, and seeing what it accepts. Do this before trusting any AI workflow:

  • Give it a document with a missing date, a wrong total, or two conflicting figures.
  • If it fills the gap silently instead of flagging it, add "Flag missing or inconsistent data; never guess" to your instructions.
  • For costs: send simple tasks to a smaller model and keep prompts short. Extra context is the most common waste.

Contradictions & blind spots

Bond rally or bond bear?

TBL sees a flight to Treasuries; Dalio says the bear market in bonds has further to run. Both can hold: a short rally inside a longer decline. Claude

Is Wall Street buying Bitcoin?

JPMorgan ranks it above stocks for the next decade, and ETFs took in $301m net. Yet Thursday's outflow was the biggest since June and price fell below $80k.

Is Hormuz open?

Iran says shipping will be managed differently from now on; Washington keeps a blockade and talks of new strikes. No published plan bridges the two.

Is France a crisis?

Bond markets priced the worst spread since 2011; Moody's chose not to downgrade. Ratings agencies lag markets, but they also see the budget numbers.

Missed by the newsletters this week: the Federal Council race, the Moody's decision on France, Starlink being throttled over Ukraine, and the Houthi campaign against Saudi airports, which none of the subscriptions treated as a story of its own.
